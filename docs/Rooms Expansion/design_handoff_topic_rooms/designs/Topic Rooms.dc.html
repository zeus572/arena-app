D · Showing uncertainty

Eight statuses (PRD 01 §6.8) is a lot to teach. Traffic lights would read as partisan and as a verdict. This is a shape-and-ink system instead: it survives greyscale, screen readers get the word, and "disputed" literally looks like two sides.

1m The status system One mark, one word, everywhere. Never a colour alone.

Evidence marks

Ink density = how much the evidence supports it

Confirmed

Multiple independent sources, or a primary document. Solid.

Strongly supported

Good evidence, no direct contradiction, but not independently confirmed.

Plausible but unresolved

Could be true; the evidence that would settle it does not exist yet.

Disputed

Credible sources directly contradict each other. The mark is split because the record is.

Unsupported

Circulating widely with no evidence behind it. Hatched, not filled.

False

Evidence shows it is not true. We keep it in the ledger rather than deleting it.

Outdated

Was accurate. Something changed. The date it went stale is shown.

Prediction

A statement about the future. Not a fact, however confidently it was said.

In running prose

The strikes hit three enrichment facilities , and officials say the program was set back by years — a figure another assessment puts at months. Iran has said it will not return to talks under pressure .

Marks sit after the clause they qualify, are focusable, and read as "disputed claim, four assessments — open evidence" to a screen reader. Hovering dims everything except statements at that status.

What the strikes hit, and what is still disputed

Three enrichment facilities were struck on 19 July. Whether that ended Iran's programme or delayed it is the question four assessments cannot agree on.

Contains a disputed central claim Event 19 Jul · reviewed 3 Aug · r.31 Describes damage to facilities; no graphic content

Before you know · 1 question

Under U.S. law, who decides whether a strike like this required Congress's approval?

The Supreme Court Congress, by passing a resolution The President's own legal counsel Nobody, definitively — it has never been settled

No wrong answer is punished. We show what most readers picked after you commit — and 68% of them pick "Congress".

What happened

On 19 July, U.S. aircraft and standoff munitions struck three declared enrichment facilities. The operation was announced the same day.

Iran launched missiles at two regional bases within 36 hours. Both had been evacuated; no deaths were reported by either side.

How badly the facilities were damaged is disputed. Officials say years; one agency assessment says months.

This is the single most consequential open question in the story. Open the evidence trail

There has been no strike by either side in eleven days.

Why it matters

Legal

It reopens a fifty-year argument about whether the President can start hostilities without a vote.

Institutional

The Senate now has to vote. Whatever it decides becomes precedent for the next administration.

Financial

A $4.1B supplemental has been requested. Requested — not approved, not spent. Money Trail

Human

Damage to facilities near populated areas has raised contamination questions that are not yet answerable.

Immediate

Oil rose 9% in two days, then gave most of it back. Markets priced this as contained.

Longer term

If inspectors do not return, the world loses its only independent view of the programme. That is the durable risk.

Legislative development · In: U.S.–Iran escalation · Congress & war powers 6 min · vote in 10 days

The war powers resolution the Senate is about to vote on

It would order U.S. forces out of hostilities with Iran within 30 days. It is privileged, so it cannot be buried. It is also expected to be vetoed.

What it actually does

Orders removal of forces from hostilities with Iran not authorised by Congress, within 30 days.

Carves out defence against an imminent attack — the exception that does most of the real work.

Does not cut funding, sanction anyone, or restrict intelligence activity.

Does not settle the constitutional question. It asserts one answer.

Vote before reading

Answer now. Then read both sides' strongest argument below and answer again. We show you whether you moved — and only you see it.

The strongest version of each argument

For

If a President can strike a state's nuclear infrastructure without a vote, the war power has moved permanently to one office. Congress reclaiming it here costs nothing operationally — the imminent-attack carve-out remains.

Composite of floor statements by 4 sponsors · sourced

Against

A 30-day withdrawal clock announced publicly tells an adversary exactly how long to wait. Deterrence depends on ambiguity, and the strikes were a discrete action, not a war requiring authorization.

Composite of committee testimony and 3 statements · sourced

What both sides accept: the strikes happened without a prior vote, and the War Powers Resolution has never been enforced by a court. They disagree about what follows from that.
